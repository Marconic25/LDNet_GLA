\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The wings of a transport aircraft are designed to withstand a wide range of operating conditions, but a small subset of events ends up driving the structural sizing. Among these, atmospheric gusts play a central role~\cite{hoblit1988gust,wright2007aeroelasticity}. A vertical gust hitting the wing produces a sudden change in the angle of attack, which generates a peak in the lift and a transient oscillation of the structure. The amplitude of this peak, together with the bending moment that follows, contributes to the design loads that the wing has to carry without failure, and the certification specifications prescribe the discrete gust and continuous turbulence conditions that the airframe must survive~\cite{EASA_CS25}. A wing sized only on this basis tends to be heavy, because the structure is dimensioned against a load that occurs for a very small fraction of the flight envelope.

This is the reason why Gust Load Alleviation (GLA) systems have been studied for several decades and are now part of the design of most modern transport aircraft. Active load alleviation entered service in the 1970s with the C-5A, whose ailerons were driven by accelerometer feedback to relieve the wing-root bending moment~\cite{disney1977c5a}, and later became an enabling technology for load-critical configurations such as the B-2~\cite{britt2000b2}; surveys of the applications of active control to gust alleviation and flutter suppression are given in~\cite{regan2012survey,GLA_review}. The principle is simple: if a control surface, typically a flap or an aileron, is deflected at the right time and with the right amplitude, the aerodynamic load produced by the gust can be partially cancelled, and the corresponding peak on the structure can be reduced. The benefits are that the wing can be designed against a less demanding load envelope, with a direct impact on its weight and on the fuel consumption of the aircraft, and, for the same structure, the aircraft can fly safely through more severe gust conditions~\cite{regan2012survey}.

GLA architectures differ in the information made available to the controller and in the control law that maps this information onto the control surfaces. Purely reactive feedback schemes act on the structural response measured by accelerometers and inertial sensors, and are inherently limited by the delay between the gust encounter and the measurable response of the structure~\cite{regan2012survey}. Within this class, classical proportional--integral--derivative loops on the measured accelerations have been demonstrated in the wind tunnel up to the scale of a full elastic aircraft model~\cite{zhao2023windtunnel}, linear quadratic regulators have been applied to gust alleviation on a flexible wing with piezoelectric actuation~\cite{versiani2019smartwing}, and linear quadratic Gaussian designs complement the optimal state feedback with an estimator of the aeroelastic states that cannot be measured directly~\cite{duessler2023lqg}. All these laws are synthesised on a linearised low-order model of the aeroelastic system, and their performance degrades when the true response deviates from that model; robust $H_2$ and $H_\infty$ formulations mitigate this sensitivity by enforcing performance over a prescribed set of model uncertainties, and have been validated in aeroservoelastic wind tunnel tests~\cite{ting2023preview,he2023robust}. Nonlinear and adaptive techniques relax the dependence on an accurate model in a different way: incremental nonlinear dynamic inversion replaces most of the model with acceleration measurements, so that only the control effectiveness needs to be known~\cite{wang2019indi}; dynamic control allocation distributes the alleviation command over redundant control surfaces while accounting for the actuator dynamics~\cite{hansen2022allocation}; active disturbance rejection control lumps the gust and the modelling errors into a total disturbance that an extended state observer estimates and cancels online~\cite{yue2023adrc}.

Feedback alone, however, can only react after the gust has reached the structure. Feedforward and mixed feedforward--feedback schemes add a direct measurement of the disturbance, taken at the aircraft nose by air-data sensors or ahead of the aircraft by remote sensing~\cite{alam2015mixed}: airborne Doppler lidar can measure the vertical wind field tens of metres ahead of the wing~\cite{rabadan2010lidar}, and lidar-based preview has become the reference architecture for the anticipatory alleviation of discrete gusts~\cite{fezans2017lidar,cavaliere2022estimator,fournier2022lidar}. On the control side, model predictive control exploits such a preview naturally, because the disturbance forecast enters a receding-horizon optimisation that also enforces the actuator limits explicitly~\cite{rawlings2017mpc}; it has been applied to the gust load alleviation of large transport aircraft~\cite{giesseler2012mpc}, also in combination with a linear quadratic Gaussian estimator of the unmeasured states~\cite{liu2017lqgmpc}, and preview has likewise been embedded in robust synthesis~\cite{ting2023preview}. More recently, machine learning has been brought into the control law itself, with neural network feedforward compensators trained on gust responses~\cite{zhou2024feedforward} and reinforcement learning policies commanding a morphing camber~\cite{hu2025rl}; such controllers capture the nonlinearity of the aeroelastic response, but encode the controlled dynamics implicitly in the policy and give up the explicit constraint handling of a model-based design. With the exception of these model-free schemes, all the architectures above share the same requirement: an internal model of the aeroelastic response that is accurate enough to predict the effect of the control surface and cheap enough to be evaluated in real time.

The design of an active GLA system is therefore based upon two environments. The first one is a model of the aeroelastic response of the wing to the gust, accurate enough to capture the relevant physics, namely the unsteady aerodynamics, the structural dynamics and the coupling between the two. The second one is a control strategy that uses this model to compute, at every instant, how much the flap should deflect in order to minimise a given performance metric. Each of these two ingredients can be addressed at different levels of fidelity, and the two choices are not independent. A high-fidelity model, based on a full Computational Fluid Dynamics solver coupled with a structural integrator in a Fluid-Structure Interaction (FSI) framework~\cite{bazilevs2013csfi}, captures the physics in great detail but has a computational cost that is incompatible with any real-time use, and is also unsuitable for control design, because it does not provide a compact representation of the system that the controller can act upon. Low-order models obtained from linear unsteady aerodynamic theories~\cite{Theodorsen,hoblit1988gust}, on the other hand, are fast enough to be used in a control loop, but rely on assumptions of small amplitudes, attached flow and inviscid behaviour that are not always justified during a strong gust event, especially when the flap is deflected by a large angle.

In recent years, this gap between high-fidelity simulations and control-oriented models has been progressively filled by data-driven surrogate models~\cite{brunton2020ml}. The idea is to run a limited number of high-fidelity simulations, collect the resulting data, and train a reduced model that learns the dynamics of the system from these data. Once trained, the surrogate replaces the original solver in any task for which speed and differentiability matter, including parametric studies, uncertainty quantification and, of particular interest here, model-based control. Many architectures have been proposed for this purpose, ranging from classical projection-based reduced-order models~\cite{BennerGugercinWillcox2015}, such as Proper Orthogonal Decomposition combined with a Galerkin projection~\cite{QuarteroniManzoniNegri2016}, which have been applied to build reduced aeroelastic models for gust load alleviation and active flutter suppression~\cite{ZhangChenYe2014,BertolinEtAl2018}, to autoencoder-based models in which a neural network compresses the state of the system into a low-dimensional latent space and a second network propagates it in time~\cite{LeeCarlberg2020,FrescaDedeManzoni2021}, with recent applications to nonlinear flutter prediction on airfoil and wing configurations~\cite{MogharabinEpureanuGhadami2025,WangEtAl2025}. Among the most recent contributions, Latent Dynamics Networks (LDNet)~\cite{Regazzoni} learn a low-dimensional latent representation and its dynamics directly from input-output trajectories, without the need for a separate autoencoder, and have shown a remarkable accuracy on a range of spatio-temporal problems with a small number of latent variables.

The present work fits in this picture. The aim is to investigate whether a Latent Dynamics Network can be used as the internal model of an active GLA system for a two-dimensional airfoil with a hinged trailing-edge flap. The full-order model of reference is a coupled FSI solver, in which an OpenFOAM Computational Fluid Dynamics simulation is coupled with a Python structural integrator through a partitioned scheme. A dataset of FSI simulations is generated by varying the gust intensity, the gust wavelength and the flap deflection schedule. An LDNet is then trained on this dataset and is used to predict the response of the wing to unseen gust and flap inputs. The accuracy of the surrogate is assessed against the FSI reference on the integral aerodynamic and structural quantities that are relevant for the control design. The trained LDNet is finally embedded in a model predictive controller fed by a fused lidar preview of the incoming gust, and the closed-loop performance is compared with the open-loop response of the same aeroelastic system. The results show that the surrogate is accurate enough to be used in a control loop and that the resulting controller reduces the peak lift coefficient produced by the gust by a significant margin, with a flap deflection that remains within realistic actuator limits.

This work is organised as follows. In \cref{sec:model} the model is decomposed into subsystems to facilitate its description: the aeroelastic model of the airfoil, the Latent Dynamics Network that surrogates the aerodynamic coefficients and the architecture and cost function of the gust load alleviation controller. \Cref{sec:methods} presents the methods: the time discretisation of the closed-loop system, the simulation campaign that generates the training dataset, and the training of the LDNet. \Cref{sec:results} reports the results, from the accuracy of the trained surrogate to the closed-loop performance over the certification gust envelope and its robustness to measurement noise and systematic errors. \Cref{sec:conclusion} draws the conclusions. The numerical solution of the full-order model is collected in \cref{app:fom} and its verification and validation in \cref{app:vv}.
