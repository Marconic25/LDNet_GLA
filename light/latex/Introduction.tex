\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The wings of a transport aircraft are designed to withstand a wide range of operating conditions, but a small subset of events ends up driving the structural sizing. Among these, atmospheric gusts play a central role. A vertical gust hitting the wing produces a sudden change in the angle of attack, which generates a peak in the lift and a transient oscillation of the structure. The amplitude of this peak, together with the bending moment that follows, contributes to the design loads that the wing has to carry without failure. A wing sized only on this basis tends to be heavy, because the structure is dimensioned against a load that occurs for a very small fraction of the flight envelope.

This is the reason why Gust Load Alleviation (GLA) systems have been studied for several decades and are now part of the design of most modern transport aircraft \cite{GLA_review}. The reason is simple: if a control surface, typically a flap or an aileron, is deflected at the right time and with the right amplitude, the aerodynamic load produced by the gust can be partially cancelled, and the corresponding peak on the structure can be reduced. The benefits are that the wing can be designed against a less demanding load envelope, with a direct impact on its weight and on the fuel consumption of the aircraft, and, for the same structure, the aircraft can fly safely through more severe gust conditions.

The design of an active GLA system is based upon two environments. The first one is a model of the aeroelastic response of the wing to the gust, accurate enough to capture the relevant physics, namely the unsteady aerodynamics, the structural dynamics and the coupling between the two. The second one is a control strategy that uses this model to compute, at every instant, how much the flap should deflect in order to minimise a given performance metric. Each of these two ingredients can be addressed at different levels of fidelity, and the two choices are not independent. A high-fidelity model, based on a full Computational Fluid Dynamics solver coupled with a structural integrator in a Fluid-Structure Interaction (FSI) framework, captures the physics in great detail but has a computational cost that is incompatible with any real-time use, and is also unsuitable for control design, because it does not provide a compact representation of the system that the controller can act upon. Low-order models obtained from linear aerodynamic theories, on the other hand, are fast enough to be used in a control loop, but rely on assumptions of small amplitudes, attached flow and inviscid behaviour that are not always justified during a strong gust event, especially when the flap is deflected by a large angle.

In recent years, this gap between high-fidelity simulations and control-oriented models has been progressively filled by data-driven surrogate models. The idea is to run a limited number of high-fidelity simulations, collect the resulting data, and train a reduced model that learns the dynamics of the system from these data. Once trained, the surrogate replaces the original solver in any task for which speed and differentiability matter, including parametric studies, uncertainty quantification and, of particular interest here, model-based control. Many architectures have been proposed for this purpose, ranging from classical reduced basis methods such as Proper Orthogonal Decomposition combined with a Galerkin projection~\cite{QuarteroniManzoniNegri2016}, which have been applied to build reduced aeroelastic models for gust load alleviation and active flutter suppression~\cite{ZhangChenYe2014,BertolinEtAl2018}, to autoencoder-based models in which a neural network compresses the state of the system into a low-dimensional latent space and a second network propagates it in time~\cite{FrescaDedeManzoni2021}, with recent applications to nonlinear flutter prediction on airfoil and wing configurations~\cite{MogharabinEpureanuGhadami2025,WangEtAl2025}. Among the most recent contributions, Latent Dynamics Networks (LDNet) \cite{Regazzoni} learn a low-dimensional latent representation and its dynamics directly from input-output trajectories, without the need for a separate autoencoder, and have shown a remarkable accuracy on a range of spatio-temporal problems with a small number of latent variables.

The present work fits in this picture. The aim is to investigate whether a Latent Dynamics Network can be used as the internal model of an active GLA system for a two-dimensional airfoil with a hinged trailing-edge flap. The full-order model of reference is a coupled FSI solver, in which an OpenFOAM Computational Fluid Dynamics simulation is coupled with a Python structural integrator through a partitioned scheme. A dataset of FSI simulations is generated by varying the gust intensity, the gust wavelength and the flap deflection schedule. An LDNet is then trained on this dataset and is used to predict the response of the wing to unseen gust and flap inputs. The accuracy of the surrogate is assessed against the FSI reference both on the full pressure field and on the integral aerodynamic and structural quantities that are relevant for the control design. The trained LDNet is finally embedded in a Optimal Controller - a model-based control strategy - and the closed-loop performance is compared with the open-loop response of the same FSI system. The results show that the surrogate is accurate enough to be used in a control loop and that the resulting controllers are able to reduce the peak coefficient of lift produced by the gust by a significant margin, with a flap deflection that remains within realistic actuator limits.
