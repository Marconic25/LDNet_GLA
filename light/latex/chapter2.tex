\section{Latent Dynamics Networks: problem formulation}

The modelling of systems with spatio-temporal dynamics --- typical, for instance, of
aeroelasticity --- traditionally relies on the numerical solution of partial differential
equations (PDEs). Although rigorous, this physics-based approach entails high computational
costs and assumes complete knowledge of the governing physical principles and boundary
conditions. In many-query contexts --- such as sensitivity analysis, uncertainty
quantification, or real-time control --- this burden can become prohibitive.

Data-driven methods offer an alternative paradigm: the system evolution is learned directly
from a dataset of observations, without requiring the explicit form of the underlying
governing equations. Consider an output field $\mathbf{y}: \Omega \times [0,T] \to
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathbf{y}}}$, defined over a spatial domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$
and a time horizon $[0,T]$, whose evolution is driven by an input $\mathbf{u}: [0,T] \to
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathbf{u}}}$ (time-dependent signals and/or scalar parameters). The
objective is to learn the map $\mathbf{u} \mapsto \mathbf{y}$, i.e.\ to infer
$\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x},t)$ for inputs not observed during training.

Latent Dynamics Networks (LDNets) address this need through a latent-state representation
of the system. Rather than operating in a high-dimensional space --- as required by
classical reduction techniques (POD, convolutional auto-encoders) --- LDNets automatically
discover a compact encoding of the state in terms of a latent variable vector $\mathbf{s}(t)
\in \mathbb{R}^{d_s}$. These variables are not defined a priori, but emerge during training
as a representation functional to both the spatial field reconstruction and its temporal
evolution.

The latent-state dynamics are governed by a system of ordinary differential equations
coupled with a meshless reconstruction network:
\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
\dot{\mathbf{s}}(t) = \mathcal{NN}_{\mathrm{dyn}}\big(\mathbf{s}(t), \mathbf{u}(t);
    \mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{dyn}}\big), & t \in (0,T], \\[4pt]
\mathbf{s}(0) = \mathbf{0}, \\[4pt]
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x},t) = \mathcal{NN}_{\mathrm{rec}}\big(\mathbf{s}(t),
    \mathbf{u}(t), \mathbf{x}; \mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{rec}}\big), & \mathbf{x} \in \Omega,\;
    t \in [0,T],
\end{cases}
\label{eq:LDNet_system}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{NN}_{\mathrm{dyn}}$ and $\mathcal{NN}_{\mathrm{rec}}$ are two
fully-connected neural networks (FCNNs) with trainable parameters $\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{dyn}}$
and $\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{rec}}$. The initial condition $\mathbf{s}(0)=\mathbf{0}$ is assumed
without loss of generality, as the latent state is a hidden quantity learned by the model.

The reconstruction network $\mathcal{NN}_{\mathrm{rec}}$ is queried independently for each
spatial point $\mathbf{x} \in \Omega$, without resorting to a predefined grid. This enables
weight sharing across all points in the domain, reducing the parameter count and mitigating
overfitting. The absence of an explicit high-dimensional state representation keeps training
lightweight and allows generalisation beyond training time horizons. When the output field
depends instantaneously on the input, the reconstruction network can be extended to include
$\mathbf{u}(t)$ among its arguments, as in equation~\eqref{eq:LDNet_system}; this choice is
assessed through cross-validation and is particularly relevant when modelling aerodynamic
forces that depend instantaneously on angle of attack or control surface deflections.


\subsection{LDNet architecture: inputs, outputs, and latent variables}

An LDNet is composed of two FCNNs: the dynamics network $\mathcal{NN}_{\mathrm{dyn}}$ and
the reconstruction network $\mathcal{NN}_{\mathrm{rec}}$. Their interplay defines a map from
time-dependent inputs to spatio-temporal output fields through a low-dimensional latent state
$\mathbf{s}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_s}$.

Tables~\ref{tab:ldnet_inputs} and~\ref{tab:ldnet_outputs} list the quantities fed to and
produced by each sub-network. The input signals $\mathbf{u}(t)$ collect all time-varying
quantities; the input parameter $U_\infty$ is time-constant and characterises the operating
condition. All inputs are independently normalised to the approximate range $[-1,1]$ via
affine scaling based on their empirical or physical bounds.

The system history is encoded in $\mathbf{s}(t)$, governed by the ODE
\[
\dot{\mathbf{s}}(t) = \Delta t_{\mathrm{ref}}^{-1} \,
    \widetilde{\mathcal{NN}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}\big(\mathbf{s}(t), \mathbf{u}(t), \mathbf{p};
    \mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{dyn}}\big),
\]
where $\widetilde{\mathcal{NN}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}$ is a FCNN with $\tanh$ activations
(e.g.\ $7$--$7$--$d_s$ neurons) and $\Delta t_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is a characteristic time
scale. The ODE is discretised by forward Euler with time step $\Delta t$. The initial
condition is fixed to $\mathbf{s}(0) = \mathbf{0}$; the network learns an internal
representation that makes this origin compatible with the initial state of the training
trajectories. When data are generated about a steady trim condition $\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{eq}}$,
the network is centred so that $\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{0}$ is a fixed point by construction:
\[
\mathcal{NN}_{\mathrm{dyn}}(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{u}) =
    \widetilde{\mathcal{NN}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{u}) -
    \widetilde{\mathcal{NN}}_{\mathrm{dyn}}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{eq}}).
\]

The reconstruction network is queried independently for each spatial point $\mathbf{x}$ and
returns
\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x},t) = \mathbf{y}_0 + \mathbf{y}_w \odot
    \widetilde{\mathcal{NN}}_{\mathrm{rec}}\big(\mathbf{s}(t), \mathbf{u}(t), \mathbf{x};
    \mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{rec}}\big),
\]
where $\widetilde{\mathcal{NN}}_{\mathrm{rec}}$ is a FCNN (e.g.\ four hidden layers of $24$
neurons with $\tanh$), $\mathbf{y}_0$ and $\mathbf{y}_w$ are affine denormalisation
parameters, and $\odot$ denotes the Hadamard product. Because $\mathcal{NN}_{\mathrm{rec}}$ shares weights across all spatial
queries, the model never explicitly constructs a high-dimensional state vector, keeping the
parameter count independent of the spatial resolution.





\subsection{Dataset generation and preprocessing}

The training of an LDNet requires a dataset of input--output pairs obtained from either
high-fidelity numerical simulations or experimental measurements. In the present aeronautical
context, the data are generated by a full-order model (FOM)---a coupled FSI
solver---that resolves the spatio-temporal flowfield and the structural response for a finite
set of operating conditions. Each sample consists of time-dependent input signals
$\mathbf{u}(\tau)$, output signals $\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{sig}}(\tau)$, and output fields
$\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{fld}}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\tau)$ sampled at discrete times and spatial
points.

The dataset is organised into three families of simulations, distinguished by the type of
excitation applied to the aeroelastic system (Table~\ref{tab:dataset_families}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Family A (gust only): 30 samples with varying gust amplitude $R$ and duration
          $T_g$, but no actuation ($\delta \equiv 0$). The gust peak velocity $W_{g0}$
          ranges from $8.7$ to $47.8$~m/s.
    \item Family B (flap only): 46 samples with prescribed flap deflections (no gust). The
          flap rate and maximum deflection are swept to cover the actuator envelope.
    \item Family Cc (gust + flap): 70 samples combining both gust excitation and active flap
          control, with coupled parameter sweeps.
\end{itemize}
The complete dataset comprises 146 simulations, partitioned into training (100), validation
(23), and test (23) sets. The split is stratified by family to ensure that each excitation
regime is represented in all subsets.

Before training, the raw FOM data are resampled to a uniform time step $\Delta t = 0.002$~s.
Each scalar quantity---input parameters, signals, and spatial coordinates---is independently
normalised to the approximate interval $[-1,1]$ via affine scaling based on the physical
bounds reported in the dataset metadata. The time step is scaled by a reference time constant
$\Delta t_{\mathrm{ref}} = 0.002$~s, which enters the latent dynamics discretisation.

\subsection{Normalisation}

Each scalar quantity entering the networks is mapped to the interval $[-1,1]$ by the
affine transformation
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\alpha} = \frac{\alpha - \alpha_0}{\alpha_w}, \qquad
\alpha_0 = \frac{\alpha_{\min}+\alpha_{\max}}{2}, \qquad
\alpha_w  = \frac{\alpha_{\max}-\alpha_{\min}}{2},
\label{eq:norm_affine}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{\min}$ and $\alpha_{\max}$ are the physical bounds computed from the training
dataset. The transformation is applied independently to every input signal ($h$, $\dot{h}$,
$\alpha$, $\dot{\alpha}$, $\delta$, $W_\mathrm{gust}$), to the input parameter $U_\infty$,
and to the aerodynamic output signals ($F_y$, $M_z$). The spatial query coordinate
$\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{query}$ is scaled using the bounds of the spatial domain. The
integration time step is divided by the reference time constant
$\Delta t_\mathrm{ref} = 0.002$~s before entering $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{dyn}$, which keeps
the network output dimensionally consistent with the inverse of time. The latent states
$\mathbf{s}$ are not pre-normalised; when the hyperparameters are well tuned the optimiser
produces latent trajectories that remain approximately within a unit ball.

The aerodynamic outputs $F_y$ and $M_z$ have skewed, long-tailed distributions across the
training set, driven by the large steady trim loads relative to the gust-induced excursions.



\subsection{Training and loss function}

$\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{dyn}$ and $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{rec}$ are trained simultaneously by
empirical risk minimisation on the $|\mathcal{S}_\mathrm{train}| = 100$ training
trajectories. Under teacher forcing, both networks receive the ground-truth structural state
$\mathbf{x}_t^\mathrm{data}$ as input at every step. This means that during training the
network never rolls out its own predictions: at each step $t$ the true state
$\mathbf{x}_t^\mathrm{data}$ is fed into the network, the loss is computed on the
one-step prediction, and then the process restarts from the next ground-truth state
$\mathbf{x}_{t+\Delta t}^\mathrm{data}$. Consequently, the loss optimises only the
one-step mapping $(\mathbf{s}_t,\, \mathbf{u}_t^\mathrm{data}) \mapsto \tilde{\mathbf{y}}_t$,
where $\mathbf{u}_t^\mathrm{data} = [\mathbf{x}_t^\mathrm{data},\,\delta_t,\,W_t]^\mathsf{T}$
contains the ground-truth structural states. Because the rollout chain is broken at every
step, the gradient of this loss contains no information about how a load prediction error
$\varepsilon_t = \tilde{F}_{y,t} - F_{y,t}^\mathrm{data}$ propagates through the structural
ODE~\eqref{eq:struct_ode}: the chain $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_t \to \mathbf{x}_{t+\Delta t}^\mathrm{rollout}$
is absent from the computational graph because $\mathbf{x}_{t+\Delta t}$ is always replaced by
$\mathbf{x}_{t+\Delta t}^\mathrm{data}$ before the next step. At inference, however, this feedback
is no longer suppressed: the network must consume its own predictions (or the plant state
evolved from them), so any prediction error $\varepsilon_t$ produces a structural state
deviation $\Delta\mathbf{x}_{t+\Delta t} = \mathbf{x}_{t+\Delta t}^\mathrm{rollout} - \mathbf{x}_{t+\Delta t}^\mathrm{data}$,
which shifts the network input at step $t+\Delta t$ outside the training distribution. This
distribution shift generates a larger error at the next step, and the error compounds over
time, destabilising the loop. This is the classical \emph{exposure bias} problem: the model
is never trained on its own errors and therefore lacks the corrective feedback needed to
stay on trajectory during closed-loop operation.

A second, independent failure mode arises from the redundancy of $\delta_t$ and
$W_{\mathrm{gust},t}$ as network inputs when ground-truth structural states are supplied.
The CFD trajectory $\mathbf{x}_t^\mathrm{data}$ already encodes the integrated effect of
gust and flap through the preceding aeroelastic history: the incidence
$\alpha_t^\mathrm{data}$ and its rate $\dot\alpha_t^\mathrm{data}$ reflect the quasi-steady
aerodynamic response to both $W_\mathrm{gust}$ and $\delta$ accumulated over all previous
steps. In other words, the state vector is a sufficient statistic for the aerodynamic
forces, so the reconstruction network can minimise the teacher-forced loss by learning
$F_y \approx f(\mathbf{x}_t^\mathrm{data})$ alone, treating $\delta_t$ as a redundant
input. Because the loss can be driven to a low value without using $\delta_t$, the
gradients routed through the latent dynamics that should carry the causal effect of the
control surface vanish. The network therefore never learns to attribute changes in load to
changes in flap deflection. Models trained to low teacher-forced loss are consequently
\emph{flap-blind} in closed-loop operation: their load predictions are insensitive to
$\delta$, rendering GLA control ineffective, even though one-step replay on training data
appears accurate.

The loss is therefore formulated in rollout mode: the 2-DOF aeroelastic ODE is
integrated from the network's own predicted loads $(F_y,\,M_z)$ over a horizon of
$N_\mathrm{rollout} = 800$ steps, with $\delta$ and $W_\mathrm{gust}$ treated as exogenous
signals taken from the data. The aerodynamic outputs $F_y$ and $M_z$ are scalars
reconstructed at the single fixed query point $\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{query}$, so the spatial
summation present in the general LDNet formulation collapses to one term per step. The
per-step discrepancy is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}, \mathbf{y}) =
    \frac{\|\tilde{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}\|^2}{y_\mathrm{norm}^2}
    + w_\mathrm{dir}
    \left\|
        \frac{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}}{\epsilon + \|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|}
        - \frac{\mathbf{y}}{\epsilon + \|\mathbf{y}\|}
    \right\|^2,
\label{eq:discrepancy}
\end{equation}
with $y_\mathrm{norm}$ a reference magnitude and $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$ to prevent division
by zero. The first term controls magnitude accuracy; the second penalises angular deviation,
relevant for vector outputs in regions of small magnitude. This goal-oriented extension of
the standard quadratic metric follows the approach of Regazzoni et al.~\cite{Regazzoni2024};
for the scalar outputs $F_y$ and $M_z$, setting $w_\mathrm{dir} = 0$ recovers the standard
normalised mean-square error. The rollout loss is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{rollout} =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_\mathrm{train}|}
    \sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}_\mathrm{train}}
    \frac{1}{N_\mathrm{rollout}}
    \sum_{t=0}^{T_\mathrm{rollout}}
    \bigg(
        \left\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t^{\mathrm{ro},(i)} - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t^{\mathrm{data},(i)}\right\|^2
        + \mathcal{E}\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_t^{(i)},\, \mathbf{y}_t^{\mathrm{data},(i)}\right)
    \bigg) \\
    {}+ \alpha_\mathrm{dyn}\,\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{w}_\mathrm{dyn})
    + \alpha_\mathrm{rec}\,\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{w}_\mathrm{rec}),
\end{split}
\label{eq:rollout_loss}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha_\mathrm{dyn}$ and $\alpha_\mathrm{rec}$ are $L_2$-regularisation weights
and $\mathcal{R}$ denotes Tikhonov regularisation (mean of squared weights). Both the
state and load errors are evaluated in their respective normalised coordinates
(cf.~\eqref{eq:norm_affine}), which implicitly weights each degree of freedom by
$\alpha_{w,i}^{-2}$ and places the physically disparate quantities
$h$~[m], $\dot{h}$~[m\,s$^{-1}$], $\alpha$~[rad], $\dot{\alpha}$~[rad\,s$^{-1}$]
on a common dimensionless scale. The superscript $\mathrm{data}$ labels quantities drawn
from the CFD dataset; the superscript $\mathrm{ro}$ labels quantities produced by the
closed-loop simulation. The rollout structural state
$\mathbf{x}_t^{\mathrm{ro},(i)} = [h_t,\,\dot{h}_t,\,\alpha_t,\,\dot{\alpha}_t]^\mathsf{T}$
is obtained by integrating the 2-DOF equations of motion
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}_\mathrm{aug}
\begin{pmatrix}\ddot{h}\\\ddot{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}
+ \mathbf{D}
\begin{pmatrix}\dot{h}\\\dot{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}
+ \mathbf{K}
\begin{pmatrix}h\\\alpha\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix}-F_y\\M_z\end{pmatrix} + \mathbf{f}_\delta(\dot{\delta},\ddot{\delta}),
\label{eq:struct_ode}
\end{equation}
with the Dormand--Prince fifth-order Runge--Kutta scheme (RK45)~\cite{dormand1980}
driven by the loads $(F_y,\,M_z)$ predicted by the LDNet at each step.
$\mathbf{M}_\mathrm{aug}$ is the $2\times 2$ augmented mass matrix
accounting for the coupled wing--flap inertia; $\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ are diagonal
structural damping and stiffness matrices; $\mathbf{f}_\delta(\dot{\delta},\ddot{\delta})$
collects the generalised forces arising from the prescribed flap kinematics (Coriolis and
inertial coupling). The integrator matches the standalone solver used to generate the CFD
data, which calls \texttt{scipy.integrate.solve\_ivp}~\cite{virtanen2020scipy} with
method \texttt{'RK45'}, \texttt{rtol=1e-8}, \texttt{atol=1e-10}, and
$\texttt{max\_step}=2\Delta t$; the aerodynamic loads are held constant over each step.

The stepper $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ integrates the 2-DOF equations of motion
over one time interval $\Delta t$ using the Dormand--Prince
RK45 scheme~\cite{dormand1980}.  Defining the first-order state
$\mathbf{x} = [h,\,\dot{h},\,\alpha,\,\dot{\alpha}]^\mathsf{T}$, the
structural right-hand side is
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},\,F_y,\,M_z)
  = \begin{bmatrix} \dot{h} \\ \ddot{h} \\ \dot{\alpha} \\ \ddot{\alpha}
    \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
  \begin{bmatrix} \ddot{h} \\ \ddot{\alpha} \end{bmatrix}
  = \mathbf{M}^{-1}
    \begin{bmatrix}
      -F_y - D_h\dot{h} - K_h h \\
       M_z - D_\alpha\dot{\alpha} - K_\alpha\alpha
    \end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:struct_rhs}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{M} = \bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} M_{hh} & M_{h\alpha} \\
M_{h\alpha} & M_{\alpha\alpha}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$ is the structural
mass matrix and $D_h,\,D_\alpha,\,K_h,\,K_\alpha$ are the modal damping
and stiffness coefficients.
The stepper evaluates the six Dormand--Prince stages
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{k}_1 &= \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_t,\;F_{y,t},\;M_{z,t}), \notag\\
  \mathbf{k}_2 &= \mathbf{f}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t
                    + \tfrac{\Delta t}{5}\mathbf{k}_1,\;
                    F_{y,t},\;M_{z,t}\right), \notag\\
  \mathbf{k}_3 &= \mathbf{f}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t
                    + \Delta t\!\left(\tfrac{3}{40}\mathbf{k}_1
                    + \tfrac{9}{40}\mathbf{k}_2\right),\;
                    F_{y,t},\;M_{z,t}\right), \notag\\
  \mathbf{k}_4 &= \mathbf{f}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t
                    + \Delta t\!\left(\tfrac{44}{45}\mathbf{k}_1
                    - \tfrac{56}{15}\mathbf{k}_2
                    + \tfrac{32}{9}\mathbf{k}_3\right),\;
                    F_{y,t},\;M_{z,t}\right), \notag\\
  \mathbf{k}_5 &= \mathbf{f}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t
                    + \Delta t\!\left(\tfrac{19372}{6561}\mathbf{k}_1
                    - \tfrac{25360}{2187}\mathbf{k}_2
                    + \tfrac{64448}{6561}\mathbf{k}_3
                    - \tfrac{212}{729}\mathbf{k}_4\right),\;
                    F_{y,t},\;M_{z,t}\right), \notag\\
  \mathbf{k}_6 &= \mathbf{f}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t
                    + \Delta t\!\left(\tfrac{9017}{3168}\mathbf{k}_1
                    - \tfrac{355}{33}\mathbf{k}_2
                    + \tfrac{46732}{5247}\mathbf{k}_3
                    + \tfrac{49}{176}\mathbf{k}_4
                    - \tfrac{5103}{18656}\mathbf{k}_5\right),\;
                    F_{y,t},\;M_{z,t}\right),
\label{eq:dp45_stages}
\end{align}
and advances the state with the fifth-order update
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,\,F_{y,t},\,M_{z,t},\,\Delta t)
  = \mathbf{x}_t
    + \Delta t\!\left(
        \tfrac{35}{384}\mathbf{k}_1
      + \tfrac{500}{1113}\mathbf{k}_3
      + \tfrac{125}{192}\mathbf{k}_4
      - \tfrac{2187}{6784}\mathbf{k}_5
      + \tfrac{11}{84}\mathbf{k}_6
    \right).
\label{eq:dp45}
\end{equation}



Two architectural choices encode prior information about the aeroelastic system. The
latent state update incorporates an exponential decay term with coefficient $\lambda$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s}_{t+\Delta t} = (1-\lambda)\,\mathbf{s}_t
    + \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta t_\mathrm{ref}}\,
    \widetilde{\mathcal{NN}}_\mathrm{dyn}\!\left(
        \mathbf{s}_t,\, \mathbf{u}_t,\, \mathbf{p};\, \mathbf{w}_\mathrm{dyn}
    \right),
\label{eq:latent_dyn}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda = 0.003$. This damped formulation biases the latent trajectory towards the
origin and prevents the unbounded drift that the undamped model ($\lambda = 0$) exhibits
during autonomous propagation, encoding the prior that the system possesses a stable
aeroelastic equilibrium. The approach differs from the exact equilibrium constraint of
Regazzoni et al.~\cite{Regazzoni2024}, in which the right-hand side of the latent ODE is
shifted by $-\widetilde{\mathcal{NN}}_\mathrm{dyn}(\mathbf{0},\,\mathbf{u}_\mathrm{eq};\,
\mathbf{w}_\mathrm{dyn})$ so that $\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{0}$ is a strict fixed point for
any weight value. The softer approach used here does not enforce an exact fixed point; a
stable latent equilibrium emerges instead from the combined effect of the rollout loss and
the decay regularisation. Rollout training and inference both start from the CFD trim state
$\mathbf{x}_0 = [h_\mathrm{tr},\,0,\,\alpha_\mathrm{tr},\,0]^\mathsf{T}$ with
$\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{0}$; no artificial pre-settling of the latent state is applied,
since doing so introduces spurious transients that disrupt the coupling between the latent
dynamics and the structural response.


\subsection{Training algorithm}
The network weights $\mathbf{w} = (\mathbf{w}_\mathrm{dyn},\,\mathbf{w}_\mathrm{rec})$
are found by minimising $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{rollout}$~\eqref{eq:rollout_loss}. Defining
the augmented state $\mathbf{z}_t = (\mathbf{s}_t,\,\mathbf{x}_t^\mathrm{ro})$, the
rollout forward pass executes the recurrence
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{z}_{t+\Delta t} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{z}_t,\,\delta_t,\,W_t;\,\mathbf{w}),
  \qquad t = 0,\ldots,N_\mathrm{rollout},
\label{eq:rollout_map}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}$ chains the latent update~\eqref{eq:latent_dyn}, the reconstruction
network $\widetilde{\mathcal{NN}}_\mathrm{rec}$, and the structural stepper $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$.
The gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{rollout}$ with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ is obtained by
back-propagation through time: the adjoint $\boldsymbol{\mu}_t$ is propagated
backward from $t = T_\mathrm{rollout}$ to $t = 0$ via
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\mu}_{t} =
      \left(\frac{\partial \mathcal{F}}{\partial \mathbf{z}_t}\right)^\mathsf{T}
      \boldsymbol{\mu}_{t+\Delta t}
      + \frac{\partial \ell_t}{\partial \mathbf{z}_t},
  \qquad \boldsymbol{\mu}_{T_\mathrm{rollout}+1} = \mathbf{0},
\label{eq:bptt}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_t$ is the $t$-th sum in~\eqref{eq:rollout_loss}, and the weight gradient
accumulates as
\[
  \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{rollout}}{\partial \mathbf{w}_\mathrm{dyn}} =
      \sum_{t=0}^{T_\mathrm{rollout}}
      \boldsymbol{\mu}_t^\mathsf{T}\,
      \frac{\partial \mathcal{F}}{\partial \mathbf{w}_\mathrm{dyn}}.
\]
Because $\mathcal{F}$ contains the structural stepper $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$, the Jacobian
$\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial\mathbf{z}_t$ has a non-zero block that links the load
prediction at step $t$ to the next structural state $\mathbf{x}_{t+\Delta t}^\mathrm{ro}$.
Under teacher forcing this block vanishes: $\mathbf{x}_{t+\Delta t}$ is read from the dataset
and is independent of $\mathbf{w}$, so the adjoint receives no signal from structural
state errors and $\mathbf{w}_\mathrm{dyn}$ cannot learn the causal influence of $\delta_t$
on the predicted loads. The gradient with respect to $\mathbf{w}_\mathrm{rec}$ is computed
by standard back-propagation without temporal unrolling.

The weights are updated in two successive stages. The first stage applies the Adam
algorithm, which tracks element-wise exponential moving averages of the gradient
$\mathbf{g}_t = \nabla_\mathbf{w}\mathcal{L}$ and of its element-wise square,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{m}_t = \beta_1\,\mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_1)\,\mathbf{g}_t, \qquad
  \mathbf{v}_t = \beta_2\,\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_2)\,(\mathbf{g}_t \odot \mathbf{g}_t),
\label{eq:adam_moments}
\end{equation}
and applies the bias-corrected parameter update
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t
      - \eta\,\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t}+\varepsilon},
  \qquad
  \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{m}_t}{1-\beta_1^t}, \quad
  \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{v}_t}{1-\beta_2^t},
\label{eq:adam}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon = 10^{-7}$ a numerical floor. The adaptive per-weight rescaling by
$\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t}$ allows a large global learning rate $\eta = 10^{-2}$ without
divergence, enabling rapid escape from poor initialisations over several hundred epochs.
The second stage switches to limited-memory BFGS (L-BFGS), a quasi-Newton method that
accumulates a low-rank approximation $\mathbf{H}_t^{-1}$ of the inverse Hessian from the
$m$ most recent parameter increments $\Delta\mathbf{w}_j = \mathbf{w}_j - \mathbf{w}_{j-1}$
and gradient increments $\Delta\mathbf{g}_j = \nabla\mathcal{L}_j - \nabla\mathcal{L}_{j-1}$,
and performs the quasi-Newton update
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t - \alpha_t\,\mathbf{H}_t^{-1}\nabla\mathcal{L}_t
\label{eq:lbfgs}
\end{equation}
with the step size $\alpha_t$ determined by a Wolfe line search. Incorporating curvature
information allows L-BFGS to converge to higher accuracy than Adam in far fewer iterations
on the smooth, deterministic full-batch loss.

For the teacher-forced model the two stages are applied sequentially from random
initialisation (Glorot uniform weights, zero biases). For the rollout model the Adam stage
is replaced by a warm-start from the converged teacher-forced checkpoint, after which
L-BFGS is applied exclusively; restarting Adam from the warm-start degrades performance by
eroding the learned flap-aware feature representation.

\subsection{Hyperparameter tuning}

The hyperparameters subject to automatic selection are the number of layers and neurons
of $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{dyn}$ and $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{rec}$, the regularisation weights
$\alpha_\mathrm{dyn}$ and $\alpha_\mathrm{rec}$, the reference time constant
$\Delta t_\mathrm{ref}$, the latent dimension $d_s$, and the leak coefficient $\lambda$.
The search uses the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) algorithm, which builds a
probabilistic model of the objective and proposes new configurations by maximising the
expected improvement. Unpromising configurations are terminated early by the Asynchronous
Successive Halving (ASHA) scheduler, which allocates a larger training budget only to
configurations competitive after a short initial phase. Hyperparameter quality is evaluated
by $K$-fold cross-validation on the normalised prediction error over the validation set.

The definitive model has $d_s = 10$ latent states and $\Delta t_\mathrm{ref} = 0.002$~s.
The dynamics network $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{dyn}$ takes as input the concatenation of the
latent state, the normalised parameter $U_\infty$, and the six input signals (total
input dimension $d_s + 7 = 17$) and has two hidden layers of 7 neurons each. The
reconstruction network $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{rec}$ takes the latent state, the six input
signals, and the two spatial coordinates (total input dimension $d_s + 8 = 18$) and has
four hidden layers of 24 neurons each. All activations are hyperbolic tangent.

\subsection{Rollout for inference}

Once trained, the LDNet operates in closed-loop rollout: the latent state $\mathbf{s}_t$
and structural state $\mathbf{x}_t$ advance using only the model's own predictions and
the exogenous inputs (flap deflection $\delta_t$ and gust velocity $W_{\mathrm{gust},t}$).

At each time step $t$ the dynamics network advances the latent state through the damped
forward-Euler scheme~(\ref{eq:latent_dyn}),
\[
\mathbf{s}_{t+\Delta t} = (1-\lambda)\,\mathbf{s}_t
    + \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta t_\mathrm{ref}}\,
    \widetilde{\mathcal{NN}}_\mathrm{dyn}\!\left(
        \mathbf{s}_t,\, \mathbf{u}_t,\, \mathbf{p};\, \mathbf{w}_\mathrm{dyn}
    \right),
\]
where $\mathbf{u}_t = [h_t,\,\dot{h}_t,\,\alpha_t,\,\dot{\alpha}_t,\,\delta_t,\,
W_{\mathrm{gust},t}]^\mathsf{T}$ and $\mathbf{p}$ contains the parameter $U_\infty$.
The reconstruction network then evaluates the aerodynamic loads,
\[
\begin{bmatrix} F_{y,t} \\ M_{z,t} \end{bmatrix}
= \mathbf{y}_0 + \mathbf{y}_w \odot
    g\!\left(
        \widetilde{\mathcal{NN}}_\mathrm{rec}\!\left(
            \mathbf{s}_t,\, \mathbf{u}_t,\, \mathbf{x}_\mathrm{query};\,
            \mathbf{w}_\mathrm{rec}
        \right)
    \right),
\]
where $g(\tilde{y}) = (\tilde{y}^3 + \beta\tilde{y})/(1+\beta)$, with $\beta = 0.05$,
is the nonlinear output layer~(\ref{eq:nl_layer}), satisfying $g(\pm 1)=\pm 1$ and
$g(0)=0$. Its gradient $g'(\tilde{y}) = (3\tilde{y}^2+\beta)/(1+\beta)$ grows with
$|\tilde{y}|$, so training errors on peak aerodynamic loads --- reached during strong
gusts --- propagate a larger gradient signal than errors near the trim condition,
biasing the network towards accurate prediction of load extremes. $\mathbf{y}_0$ and
$\mathbf{y}_w$ are the affine denormalisation constants from~(\ref{eq:norm_affine}).
The predicted loads advance the
structural state through the 2-DOF aeroelastic ODE,
\[
\mathbf{x}_{t+\Delta t} = \boldsymbol{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,\, F_{y,t},\, M_{z,t},\, \Delta t),
\]
where $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ is the structural time stepper, implementing the same
Dormand--Prince RK45 scheme~\eqref{eq:dp45} used during training.
The initial conditions are $\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ and
$\mathbf{x}_0$ equal to the pre-gust CFD trim state.
