\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section reports the results of the study. In \cref{subsec:training_results} we assess the accuracy of the trained LDNet on the held-out test set and document the hyperparameter selection. In \cref{subsec:mpc_results} we evaluate the closed-loop controller over the CS-25 discrete-gust envelope in nominal conditions, and in \cref{subsec:noise_results,subsec:systematic_results} we probe its robustness to stochastic measurement noise and to systematic sensing and model errors.

\subsection{LDNet accuracy and hyperparameter tuning}
\label{subsec:training_results}

We swept the degrees of freedom that dominate the validation loss --- the latent dimension $d_s$, the depth of $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{dyn}$ and $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{rec}$, and the composition of the input vector $\boldsymbol{\eta}$ --- while we hold the remaining hyperparameters fixed. Along each swept axis the candidate configurations are trained in multiple independent runs that differ only in the random initialisation of the weights, every run is scored by the loss on the validation set, and the run with the lowest validation loss is retained, so that the comparison is guarded against the sensitivity of the full-batch optimisation to its trajectory.

Following~\cite{Regazzoni}, accuracy is measured by the normalised root-mean-square error and by the Pearson dissimilarity $1-\rho$. For a scalar load $y \in \{F_y, M_z\}$, a component of the output $\mathbf{y}$, with prediction $\tilde{y}$ over the $N$ test samples and time steps,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NRMSE}(y) =
\frac{
\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_k \left(\tilde{y}_k - y_k\right)^2}
}{
\max_k y_k - \min_k y_k
},
\qquad
\rho(y) =
\frac{
\mathrm{cov}(\tilde{y}, y)
}{
\sigma_{\tilde{y}}\, \sigma_y
},
\label{eq:acc_metrics}
\end{equation}
so that both $\mathrm{NRMSE}$ and $1-\rho$ vanish for a perfect reconstruction. The two loads are also reported jointly as a combined value, obtained by stacking $F_y$ and $M_z$ and normalising by a single global range; since the loads carry different units (N and N\,m), this combined value is dominated by the wider-ranged $F_y$ and is quoted only as a compact summary, the per-load values remaining the primary metric.

We deliberately perform the analysis of this subsection in open loop: the networks are driven by the reference structural signals of each trajectory, in the teacher-forcing regime of \cref{subsec:training}, and we measure the reconstruction error of the loads on the held-out test set by the metrics~\eqref{eq:acc_metrics}. Decoupled from the error propagation of an autonomous rollout, this error isolates how accurately the LDNet has learned the load mapping, and it is on this learning accuracy that the selection of the inputs and of $d_s$ is based. The regularisation weights $\alpha_\mathrm{dyn}=\alpha_\mathrm{rec}=\alpha_\mathrm{reg}=3\cdot10^{-4}$ are held equal and fixed at this value throughout the sweeps below, and Tikhonov regularization of the network weights~\cite{Regazzoni} adds to the training loss a penalty term, the mean of the squared kernels of $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{dyn}$ and $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{rec}$ weighted by $\alpha_\mathrm{reg}$. Training stops once the validation loss, computed without that term, has not improved for $200$ iterations, and the weights retained are those that attained its minimum. The closed-loop rollout is instead the mode in which the LDNet is used: trained on the rollout loss~\eqref{eq:rollout_loss} with the damped latent update~\eqref{eq:latent_dyn}, the LDNet runs in rollout inside the control loop of the GLA study of \cref{subsec:mpc_results}.

\subsubsection{Input selection (Study 1)}
\label{subsubsec:input_selection}

Study~1 fixes the outputs $(F_y,M_z)$ and varies the signals fed to the networks, to
establish how much of the structural state the load reconstruction requires. Three
configurations are compared: gust and flap only $\{W,\delta\}$; the same augmented with the
plunge and pitch $\{W,\delta,h,\alpha\}$; and the full structural state
$\{W,\delta,h,\dot h,\alpha,\dot\alpha\}$, which adds the plunge and pitch rates.
\Cref{fig:input_study} reports NRMSE and $1-\rho$ against the latent dimension $d_s$ for
the three sets, aggregated over the held-out test trajectories of the three dataset families
of \cref{tab:dataset_families}, which span gust-only, flap-only and combined gust\,+\,flap
excitation. The gust-and-flap pair alone is inadequate: without the structural state the
latent dynamics must reproduce the aeroelastic response unaided, and the combined NRMSE is the
largest of the three sets.
Adding the plunge and pitch positions stabilises the reconstruction, but the four-signal set
does not improve beyond $4.0\cdot10^{-3}$ at any latent dimension. The velocity rates
$\dot h,\dot\alpha$ are what admit the accurate regime: only the full six-signal set reaches a
combined NRMSE of $1.3$--$1.4\cdot10^{-3}$. We
adopt the full six-signal set for the remainder of this work.

\subsubsection{Latent-state dimension (Study 2)}
\label{subsubsec:latent_dim}

With the six-signal input fixed, Study~2 sweeps the latent dimension over $d_s\in\{1,3,5,10\}$; for
each value two independent training runs are performed and the run with the
lowest validation loss is retained. \Cref{fig:input_study} of Study~1 and \cref{tab:latent_sweep}
report the resulting test-set accuracy. A single latent state caps the combined NRMSE at
$4.6\cdot10^{-3}$; with more states the optimiser can reach a deep minimum of the validation
loss ($\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{val}\approx 2\cdot10^{-5}$) where the combined NRMSE drops to $1.4\cdot10^{-3}$ at $d_s=3$
and $1.3\cdot10^{-3}$ at $d_s=10$, with $1-\rho\le2\cdot10^{-5}$. The deep minimum is
not reached in every run --- at $d_s=5$ neither of its two runs located it --- a residue of the
sensitivity of the full-batch optimisation to its trajectory, which the multiple-run protocol
mitigates. We set the latent dimension to $d_s=10$, the most accurate configuration, which is
also the value adopted for the closed-loop rollout (\cref{subsec:mpc_results}).

Latent dimensions above $d_s=10$, up to $d_s=20$, were also probed without further gain, and the
reason is architectural rather than statistical: with two hidden layers of seven neurons,
$\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{dyn}$ bounds the Jacobian of the latent recurrence~\eqref{eq:latent_dyn} to
rank seven, so for $d_s>7$ at least $d_s-7$ latent directions cannot be driven by the network at
all. Left undriven, their eigenvalue in the recurrence sits at $1-\lambda$ --- unity with the
decay switched off --- rather than at a value training can shape, so widening $d_s$ past that
point adds inert state, not usable dynamics. Reaching further requires widening
$\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{dyn}$ itself, examined next in \cref{subsubsec:depth} (Study~3).

\subsubsection{Network depth (Study 3)}
\label{subsubsec:depth}

Study~3 addresses the last architectural degree of freedom, the depth of the two networks. The layer widths are
held fixed at $7$ neurons for $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{dyn}$ and $24$ for $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{rec}$,
and the total number of hidden layers $L$ is swept starting from $L=6$, the depth used elsewhere
in this work, doubled twice over $L\in\{6,12,24\}$ in the fixed $1{:}2$ split between the two
networks; \cref{tab:depth_arch} lists the three configurations. Every depth is trained at the two
latent dimensions that bracket the sweep of \cref{tab:latent_sweep}, $d_s\in\{1,10\}$, each
configuration twice from independent weight initialisations so that the effect of depth can be
told apart from the run-to-run scatter of the latent sweep, with the six-signal input, the
regularized trainer and the metrics of \cref{eq:acc_metrics} used throughout; \cref{tab:depth_loads}
collects the test-set error of the twelve runs.

Doubling the depth from $L=6$ improves the open-loop reconstruction at both latent dimensions and,
more markedly, its repeatability: $L=12$ reaches the most accurate configuration of the
entire hyperparameter study. Depth does not, however, replace latent capacity — at $d_s=1$ the
deeper network still trails the shallow one at $d_s=10$ — so the latent dimension sets the
accuracy level and the depth decides how reliably the training attains it. The ladder ends at
$L=24$: a plain $\tanh$ stack of this depth, without residual connections or a depth-aware
initialisation, saturates and attenuates the gradient layer after layer~\cite{glorot2010}, and
neither seed reaches a usable minimum, undoing the gains seen at $L=12$.

\subsubsection{Field reconstruction (Study 4)}
\label{subsubsec:field_recon}

Study~4 evaluates $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{rec}$ of \labelcref{eq:reduced_system} at query
coordinates away from the airfoil surface, where it returns the distributed field
$\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}=(v_x,v_y)$ instead of the sectional loads. \Cref{fig:cc050_umag} shows the reconstruction over a full trajectory on
a combined gust\,+\,flap case with gust peak
$W_0=\SI{32.1}{\meter\per\second}$, gust duration $T_g=\SI{1.12}{\second}$ and peak flap deflection $\SI{5.27}{\degree}$. The results are largely
satisfactory: the LDNet reproduces the velocity field closely at the quiescent and
recovery instants, with the main difficulty appearing at the peak of the dynamic
response.

\label{fig:cc050_umag}

The gain that makes this reconstruction useful is cost, not accuracy on a dial: \cref{tab:cost_accuracy}
compares the LDNet field reconstruction against the full-order CFD solve that generated its
training data, at the same query resolution (the $11\,075$-node crop mesh, a full $150$-frame
trajectory). The LDNet reconstructs a full trajectory on a single CPU core in under \SI{20}{\second}, against
roughly \SI{2}{\hour} on $16$ cores for the CFD solve: three orders of magnitude faster in wall-clock time,
and almost four in core-hours. This is not a trade-off against accuracy: the retained
configuration ($d_s=1$, mean-field split, $5266$ trainable weights) is simultaneously the cheapest
and the most accurate one tried, since the reconstruction problem's intrinsic latent dimension is
$\approx1$ and every deeper or wider decoder variant tested lost to it. The LDNet is therefore not a
cheaper-but-coarser surrogate for the CFD field; at this architecture's current accuracy ceiling, it
is simply cheaper.

\subsubsection{Retained LDNet for control}
\label{subsubsec:rollout_results}

The retained LDNet combines the results of the studies above: the six-signal input of
\cref{fig:input_study} (Study~1) and $d_s=10$, the most accurate latent dimension of
\cref{subsubsec:latent_dim} (Study~2). Depth is the one hyperparameter they do not settle by themselves:
\cref{tab:depth_loads} of Study~3 found $L=12$ the most accurate configuration of the entire study, but in
the open-loop, teacher-forced measure of \cref{subsubsec:depth}. Accuracy in that regime is
not what the controller depends on, since the retained LDNet is not run teacher-forced but in
autonomous rollout, fed only its own predictions once turned loose inside the control loop. $L=6$
and $L=12$ we therefore compare directly on the rollout loss $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{rollout}$ of
\eqref{eq:rollout_loss} that the retained LDNet is actually trained and judged on. It sums the
squared structural-state error to the load term over $N_\mathrm{rollout}=800$ autonomous steps,
where each step's error shifts the next one's input away from the training distribution --- the
recurrence that motivates fine-tuning in the first place.

Two values of $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{rollout}$ mark this comparison for each depth. The
warm-start value takes the weights exactly as left by teacher-forced training, before any
rollout-specific adjustment, and evaluates them in autonomous rollout: it measures how far
teacher-forcing alone carries the LDNet toward closed-loop behaviour. The
fine-tuned value is obtained after continuing training from those same weights directly on
$\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{rollout}$, the second stage of the recipe detailed below. At the warm start
$L=12$ is already worse than $L=6$ ($\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{rollout}=0.0864$ against $0.0373$), and
fine-tuning widens rather than closes the gap: $L=6$ reaches $1.79\cdot10^{-3}$, while $L=12$
stalls at $0.0400$, more than twenty times higher, only after its BFGS optimiser aborts a line
search and is forced to restart. Neither depth reaches that value by converging onto it: both are
best-validation checkpoints along a run that the optimiser later drives away from
(\cref{fig:loss}(b)), which is why the comparison is made on the retained minimum rather than
on the value the run happens to end at. Unlike in the open-loop regime, where added depth is a free improvement
in both accuracy and repeatability, in the closed-loop regime it works against the training
objective: a more expressive one-step map is harder to keep well-behaved once composed with itself
for hundreds of steps. The retained LDNet therefore uses $L=6$: verified to be the more stable configuration under autonomous rollout, and the one that runs inside the
control loop of the GLA studies of \cref{subsec:mpc_results}.

At $L=6$, the dynamics network $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{dyn}$ has two hidden layers of $7$ neurons and
the reconstruction network $\mathcal{NN}_\mathrm{rec}$ four hidden layers of $24$ neurons, all with
$\tanh$ activation and the tail-compressing nonlinear output layer ($\beta=0.05$); the integration
and reference time steps are $\Delta t=\Delta t_\mathrm{ref}=\SI{2e-3}{\second}$. Training uses the
two-stage teacher-forced schedule of \cref{subsubsec:training_algorithm} ($400$ Adam epochs at
learning rate $10^{-2}$ followed by L-BFGS) with the weight regularization, the damped latent
update~\eqref{eq:latent_dyn} and the validation early stopping described above.
\Cref{fig:loss}(a) shows the corresponding training history. The
curve flattens over the last decade of epochs, reaching a training loss
$\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{train}\approx3.2\cdot10^{-5}$ and a validation loss
$\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{val}\approx1.3\cdot10^{-4}$ at the retained minimum near epoch $4.2\cdot10^{3}$:
the LDNet is converged rather than truncated by the iteration budget. Training and validation
track each other throughout, with the validation curve sitting slightly above the training one
once the Tikhonov term has been driven small; the absence of any late divergence between the
two confirms that the regularized high-capacity LDNet does not overfit. Unlike this teacher-forced
pretraining, whose $\sim4\cdot10^{3}$ epochs in \cref{fig:loss}(a) run until the validation-patience
rule above fires, the fine-tuning stage that follows keeps the same architecture and inputs,
but skips the Adam warm-up and runs a much shorter schedule: pure BFGS directly
on $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{rollout}$, saving the weights whenever the rollout loss improves.
\Cref{fig:loss}(b) reports that second stage. Warm-started from the teacher-forced weights, the
rollout loss falls by a factor of $21$, from $3.7\cdot10^{-2}$ to a best validation value of
$1.79\cdot10^{-3}$ at iteration $290$; past that point the validation loss drifts upward again
while the training loss keeps decreasing monotonically, so the run is stopped once the budget
of $500$ BFGS iterations is spent rather than at a plateau, and the weights retained are those
of iteration $290$. We show the two stages
separately because they optimise different objectives with different optimisers: concatenating
them on a single axis would suggest a continuity that the warm-start restart does not have.

\subsection{GLA over the discrete-gust envelope}
\label{subsec:mpc_results}

The controller is evaluated over a parametric grid of 18 gust cases that spans the
CS-25.341 discrete gust envelope: gust amplitude $W_0 \in \{10,\,20,\,30\}\,\si{\meter\per\second}$
and gust duration $T_g \in \{0.3,\,0.4,\,0.5,\,0.7,\,1.0,\,1.2\}\,\si{\second}$.
Each case is located in the aerodynamically relevant parameter space by two quantities derived
from the gust parameters of \cref{eq:gust}: the gust gradient distance $H = U_\infty T_g/2$, the
length with which CS-25.341 parametrises the discrete-gust envelope and is therefore reported in
feet, and the reduced frequency $k = \pi\,c_{\mathrm{ref}}/(U_\infty T_g)$.

The primary performance metric is the fractional reduction of the peak
lift-coefficient excursion with respect to the open-loop reference:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CLred} = \frac{\max_t |C_L^{\mathrm{OL}}(t) - C_{L,\mathrm{trim}}|
                         - \max_t |C_L^{\mathrm{CL}}(t) - C_{L,\mathrm{trim}}|}
                         {\max_t |C_L^{\mathrm{OL}}(t) - C_{L,\mathrm{trim}}|}
                    \times 100\%,
\label{eq:clred}
\end{equation}
where the maximum is taken over the gust window $t \in [0,\, T_g + \SI{0.5}{\second}]$ and
$C_{L,\mathrm{trim}}$ is the target the controller drives the lift towards, the same
reference whose deviations the MPC cost~\eqref{eq:mpc_cost} penalises: CLred is therefore
the fraction of the open-loop excursion about that target that the controller removes.
A secondary stability check excludes any tuning that causes any of
$\dot\alpha$, $\ddot\alpha$, or $\ddot h$ to exceed three times the
corresponding open-loop peak. For each gust case the effort weight
of the cost~\eqref{eq:mpc_cost} is swept over
$R \in \{10^{-4},\,3\cdot10^{-4},\,10^{-3},\,3\cdot10^{-3},\,10^{-2}\}$,
five half-decade steps, and the selected value, written $R^{\star}$, is the
one maximising CLred among those that satisfy the check. The weight is
therefore tuned case by case rather than held fixed over the envelope, and
$R^{\star}$ is reported for every case alongside CLred.
The pitch ratio, defined as the ratio of the closed-loop to open-loop peak
pitch excursion, is monitored alongside CLred to make explicit any structural
load trade-off introduced by the flap. In this nominal evaluation the sensing
chain is noise-free, so the preview vector coincides with the sampled true
gust profile~\eqref{eq:gust}.
\Cref{tab:results_setup} collects the numerical values of the structural,
aerodynamic and actuator parameters shared by every run of this paragraph;
the symbols are those of the structural model~\eqref{eq:eom}--\eqref{eq:matrices}.
The structural and flap values are adapted from the typical-section aeroelastic

\subsubsection{Performance over the discrete-gust envelope}

\Cref{fig:mpc_traces} shows the nominal closed-loop response at the two gust
cases that this work follows throughout: Test~1 ($W_0 = \SI{20}{\meter\per\second}$, $T_g = \SI{0.7}{\second}$),
the gust of \cref{fig:cfd}, and Test~2 ($W_0 = \SI{10}{\meter\per\second}$, $T_g = \SI{0.4}{\second}$), the
short gust on which the robustness studies of
\cref{subsec:noise_results,subsec:systematic_results} are run. The preview
lets the flap deploy while the gust is still building up, and the closed-loop
lift remains confined to a narrow band around trim: the peak excursion is
reduced by $91.9\%$ and $89.1\%$ respectively, with at most $\SI{8.4}{\degree}$ and
$\SI{4.2}{\degree}$ of deflection and closed-loop pitch excursions comparable to or
moderately larger than the open-loop ones (ratios $0.90$ and $1.15$).

As with the field reconstruction of \cref{subsubsec:field_recon}, the reduced-order
controller's value is a cost argument rather than an accuracy one: the full-order closed
loop is not just harder to set up, it is also several orders of magnitude more expensive to
run, which is what makes an iterative design loop on it impractical. \Cref{tab:mpc_cost_accuracy}
times both closed loops on Test~1 and Test~2: we time the ROM controller end to end
(preview, $161$-point flap search over the $N=8$ horizon, and plant integration) on a
single CPU core; we measure the FOM cost on a short, freshly-run coupling segment of the
same co-simulation used for \cref{fig:mpc_traces} ($16$ cores, coupling window aligned to
the controller's own re-planning step, \cref{subsec:mpc_arch}), rather than reconstructed
from the campaign's archived logs, which record simulated time but not wall-clock time per
coupling window. The full-order loop costs on the order of \SI{1e3}{\second} wall-clock
of CFD for every second simulated, against \SIrange{1e1}{1e2}{\second} for the
ROM on a single core: roughly $46\times$ slower in wall-clock time at Test~1, or
$\approx700\times$ in core-hours once the FOM's $16$ cores are accounted
for. Against this cost gap, the accuracy gap of \cref{fig:mpc_traces} is modest: $11.3$ and
$14.5$ points of CLred at Test~1 and Test~2, with the reduced-order controller still
beneficial by a wide margin at both. The asymmetry compounds directly in the tuning
procedure that produced \cref{tab:cs25_grid}: selecting $R^{\star}$ sweeps five effort
weights per cell on the ROM, a search that is only affordable because each evaluation costs
seconds rather than hours; repeating it on the full-order model would multiply the
already-large per-run cost fivefold, per cell, over the whole grid. The full-order loop is
therefore best used as a final check on a design point chosen on the reduced-order model,
not as the model the design loop itself runs on.

This cost comparison bounds what the reduced-order loop has been shown to do, and it is worth
stating what it has not. The reduced-order entries of \cref{tab:mpc_cost_accuracy} time an
interpreted research prototype on a single core, not an implementation written for deployment.
On the control grid of \cref{subsec:discretisation}, $\Delta t = \SI{2}{\milli\second}$, one
simulated second requires $500$ successive decisions, so the measured \SI{80.2}{\second} and
\SI{88.0}{\second} per simulated second amount to roughly \SI{160}{\milli\second} per decision,
about two orders of magnitude above the \SI{2}{\milli\second} the step itself allows. That margin is spent on the exhaustive enumeration of the
$161$ candidate deflections over the $N=8$ horizon of \cref{subsubsec:mpc_solution} and on the
per-call overhead of the interpreted implementation, not on evaluating the LDNet, whose two
networks carry $7$ and $24$ neurons per layer over $L=6$ hidden layers in total. What this work
establishes is therefore the cost reduction against the full-order model and the practicability
of an iterative design loop on the reduced one, not embedded real-time feasibility: the latter
requires a compiled implementation and a cheaper parametrisation of the optimisation problem,
reducing $G$ or replacing the exhaustive search with a hierarchical or local one, and lies
outside the scope of this work.

controller removes more than $81\%$ of the peak excursion at every amplitude,

incidence---cannot track the gust ramp regardless of the tuning. At the
long-gust corner the stability check becomes the active constraint: the
large sustained deflection required to cancel a quasi-steady gust drives the
pitch degree of freedom, whose open-loop peaks are small for slow gusts;
aggressive tunings amplify the pitch excursion (ratios up to $1.9$) and
violate the $\dot\alpha$ bound, so the selected weight rises to

whole grid the actuator never saturates ($|\delta|_{\max} \le \SI{12.2}{\degree} <
\delta_{\max}$) and no selected tuning violates the stability check; the
pitch ratio spans $0.57$--$1.47$.

\subsection{Robustness to lidar measurement noise}
\label{subsec:noise_results}

The stochastic robustness of the pipeline is assessed with both noise
models of \cref{subsec:mpc_arch}: the constant-level model drives the
amplitude sweep of this paragraph, run on Test~2, while the
range-proportional lidar model~\eqref{eq:range_prop_noise} enters the multi-case check below. The sweep applies
the measurement model~\eqref{eq:meas_model} with the constant per-sample
level $\sigma_j = \sigma$: at every control instant $t_n$ each of the
$J_{\max}$ raw samples is corrupted by its own error draw,
\begin{equation*}
y_j(t_n) = W\bigl(t_n + j\,\Delta t\bigr) + \varepsilon_{nj}, \qquad
\varepsilon_{nj} \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\,\sigma^2\bigr), \qquad
\frac{\sigma}{W_0} \in \{0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20\}\%,
\end{equation*}
with the errors drawn independently across nodes and control instants:
white noise of up to \SI{6}{\meter\per\second} per shot. The noisy samples enter the
fusion sums of \cref{eq:invvar} exactly as drawn, and the aeroelastic
system keeps advancing with the true gust $W(t)$: the noise reaches the
control loop only through the preview vector $\widehat{W}_k$ in the
horizon cost of \cref{eq:mpc_cost}. Since the outcome depends on the particular random
sequence drawn, we simulate each level six times with independent noise
realisations, and take the statistics over the six repetitions.

\Cref{fig:noise_white} reports the resulting reduction together with the
error actually delivered to the controller after fusion,
$\sigma_{\mathrm{del}} = \mathrm{std}\bigl(\widehat{W}_1(t) - W(t+\Delta t)\bigr)$,
the standard deviation of the residual on the first preview node over the
control steps of the evaluation window, averaged over the realisations.
Since each gust
value is measured $J_{\max} = 50$ times with independent errors before
reaching the wing, the inverse-variance average of \cref{eq:invvar}
shrinks the per-sample error by roughly $\sqrt{J_{\max}} \approx 7$: at
$\sigma = \SI{0.2}{\meter\per\second}$ the delivered error is $\SI{0.02}{\meter\per\second}$, about one order of
magnitude below the raw level. The noise that actually reaches the horizon
cost~\eqref{eq:mpc_cost} is therefore far smaller than the nominal
$\sigma$, and as a result the mean reduction remains within about one
percentage point of the noise-free value up to $\sigma = 10\%\,W_0 =
\SI{1}{\meter\per\second}$---the upper end of the per-shot error of airborne Doppler hardware
discussed in \cref{subsec:mpc_arch}. Only at $\sigma = 20\%\,W_0$ does the
spread across realisations open appreciably (worst realisation $82.1\%$,
none flagged).

A further check verifies that this tolerance also holds under the
range-proportional lidar model~\eqref{eq:range_prop_noise}, whose raw
line-of-sight error (\SIrange{1}{3}{\meter\per\second}) is largest relative to the gust on Test~2 of
any case in the study: with one fixed, untuned configuration
($R = 3\times 10^{-4}$), we run Test~2 under white noise at $\sigma =
2\%\,W_0$ and under this lidar model, and in both cases the mean reduction
stays within $3.5$ percentage points of the noise-free value, with no
stability flag in any realisation (noise-free/white/lidar CLred is
$89.1\%/89.1\%/85.6\%$).

\subsection{Systematic errors and model mismatch}
\label{subsec:systematic_results}

Zero-mean noise is, however, the error the fusion layer is built to remove;
systematic errors pass through it unaffected. On the sensing side they are
modelled by corrupting the raw samples of \cref{eq:meas_model} as
\begin{equation*}
y_j(t_n) = W\bigl(t_n + j\,\Delta t + \tau\bigr) + b + \varepsilon_{nj},
\end{equation*}
with one error active at a time: a calibration bias $b = \beta\,W_0$,
identical for every sample, and a time shift $\tau$ of the measured
profile, positive when the gust appears in the preview earlier
than it arrives. Since the fusion
\eqref{eq:invvar}--\eqref{eq:tikhonov} is linear in the samples, averaging
leaves both terms untouched: the delivered preview is
$\widehat{W}_k \approx W(t + k\Delta t + \tau) + b$, an error that
enters every term of the horizon cost~\eqref{eq:mpc_cost} coherently
instead of averaging down.

\Cref{fig:noise_bias_timing} sweeps the two errors, again on Test~2. In
both panels the curve is a deterministic sweep with the systematic error
alone ($\varepsilon_{nj} = 0$); at $b = \pm 5\%\,W_0$ and
$\tau = \pm\SI{10}{\milli\second}$ the white noise of the previous paragraph
($\sigma = 2\%\,W_0$, six realisations) is superimposed on the systematic
error to probe the combination of the two error classes. On the tolerated
side the noisy means reproduce the deterministic values within a fraction
of a percentage point with no flag, so the two classes do not interact.
Both axes are asymmetric. A calibration bias that over-reads the gust
degrades slowly at first, from $+90.2\%$ at $+2\%\,W_0$ to $+53.2\%$
at $+5\%\,W_0$ (no flag), and only collapses at $+10\%\,W_0$ (flagged,
mean reduction below zero), while under-reading is nearly free down to
$-10\%\,W_0$ ($+90.0\%$, no flag): an over-read gust keeps the flap
deployed against a disturbance that never materialises, driving the flap
excursion and the pitch peak up as the bias grows, until the self-induced
transient destabilises the loop. For the systematic time shift of the
preview, the tolerated range covers the entire sweep run, $\tau \in$
\SIrange{-10}{10}{\milli\second}, with no flag raised at either end, and the two directions
are not symmetric: a delayed preview does not merely avoid degrading the
reduction, it is mildly beneficial, $+2.2$ percentage points at $\SI{-10}{\milli\second}$
($+91.3\%$, peaking at $+91.6\%$ at $\SI{-5}{\milli\second}$), while an anticipated preview
of $\SI{+10}{\milli\second}$ costs $3.9$ points ($+85.2\%$); the receding horizon re-plans
from the measured state at every step, which keeps both directions well
short of the instability seen on the calibration axis over the same sweep
range.
